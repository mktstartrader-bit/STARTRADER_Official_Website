What Is An ETF? A Complete Beginner Guide To Exchange-Traded Funds

What is an ETF? Learn how exchange-traded funds work, their types, benefits, and risks before you start investing today.

STARTRADER Research DeskPublished 9 July 2026 · Updated 18 August 2026 · 25 min read
Link copied

An ETF, or exchange-traded fund, is a basket of assets traded on a stock exchange like a stock.

Ever wondered how an investor can get access to hundreds of the best companies with just a single trade?

The first step to building a diversified portfolio without buying individual stocks one by one is to understand what is an ETF. ETFs have become incredibly popular as a simple and cost-effective way to access the financial markets.

They collect money from many investors to purchase a basket of underlying assets. These assets could be stocks, bonds, commodities or a combination of various investments.

Many investors are turning to ETFs because of instant diversification, transparency and lower costs than traditional investment vehicles. They are also flexible as they trade on open stock exchanges. You can buy and sell them whenever the market is open.

Here is the full guide to everything you need to know about ETFs. We will talk about how ETFs work, the different types of ETFs and how they compare to mutual funds and index funds. We will also cover popular ETF examples, steps to invest, key risks to be aware of and common beginner mistakes to avoid.

Quick answer

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund that owns a basket of assets and is traded like a share on a stock exchange. If you are asking what is an ETF, it is a basket of investments that track indices, sectors, commodities, bonds or themes. Capital is at risk since ETF values are subject to market price movements.

What Is An ETF?

An ETF is an exchange-traded fund which are a holding of a collection of assets that trade on a stock exchange during market hours.

ETF Meaning In Simple Terms

ETF stands for exchange-traded fund. It’s a financial product that combines money from lots of investors to buy a big basket of assets. You can think of an ETF as a pre-packaged bundle of investments.

Rather than buying 500 different stocks to track the market, you could buy a single share of an ETF. That one share makes you a part-owner of the whole bundle. This makes it a lot easier for the average investor to build a diversified portfolio.

How ETFs Are Bought And Sold

You trade ETFs on public stock exchanges through a traditional brokerage platform. They trade just as one share of a company trades.

They are traded on an exchange and their prices go up and down throughout the trading day. If you want to buy an ETF at 10:30 AM, you pay the market price at that moment. This intraday trading flexibility is a major feature of the ETF format.

What ETFs Can Hold

ETFs are very flexible, and can contain almost any kind of asset. While many ETFs follow broad stock indexes, some track specific sectors such as technology or health care.

They can also hold fixed income products, commodities, currencies or a mix of these. The specific securities held are entirely a function of the fund’s stated objective. When you look at an ETF, the provider will clearly state the underlying assets the fund owns.

How ETFs Differ From Mutual Funds

ETFs and mutual funds are alike in that they both pool money from investors to purchase baskets of assets. The main difference is how they are traded and priced.

ETFs are traded continuously throughout the day at prices that vary with the market. Mutual funds, on the other hand, are priced and traded only once a day after the market closes at their Net Asset Value (NAV). We will talk about the more profound differences in the ETF vs mutual fund section later on in this guide.

How Do ETFs Work?

ETFs pool together underlying assets and issue fund shares that can be bought and sold on an exchange by investors.

The ETF Provider Creates The Fund

It all begins when an ETF provider or fund manager decides to create a new ETF. The provider selects a particular index, sector or strategy that the fund will track.

The provider then buys the underlying assets that make up that basket. After the assets are secured, the provider issues shares of the fund. These shares are fractional ownership of the overall pool of assets. This is the creation phase and this is where you begin to understand how ETF investing works.

Investors Buy ETF Shares On An Exchange

The ETF shares, once created, are listed on a public stock exchange. These shares can then be purchased by ordinary investors through their brokerage accounts.

When you buy an ETF share, you don’t directly own the individual stocks or bonds in the basket. You are a part owner of the fund itself. Behind the scenes, the fund manager does all the buying, selling and rebalancing of the individual assets.

NAV And Market Price

Actually, you need to know two different values of an ETF. The first is the Net Asset Value (NAV) which is the total value of all the assets underlying the fund.

The second is the market price. This is the price at which the ETF is currently trading in the exchange. Normally, the market price is very near the NAV. But in periods of high volatility the market price can for a short period of time be a little bit above or below the real value of the asset.

Authorized Participants Help Keep Prices Aligned

ETFs rely on special financial institutions known as authorized participants or APs to maintain the market price near the NAV. APs are allowed to create or redeem ETF shares.

Similarly, when the price of an ETF is a great deal higher than its NAV, the APs will buy new shares, thus putting downward pressure on the price. If the share price drops too far, they’ll come out with more to raise the price. This behind-the-scenes operation helps maintain fair and accurate ETF pricing.

What Are The Main Types Of ETFs?

There are many types of ETFs, including equity ETFs, bond ETFs, commodity ETFs, leveraged ETFs, inverse ETFs, and thematic ETFs.

Equity ETFs

Equity ETFs are funds that only hold stocks of companies that are publicly traded. They are the most common kind of ETF on the market.

These funds can track broad market indexes, like the S&P 500, to offer broad exposure to the stock market. They can also be targeted at specific sectors, geographical areas or company sizes. Equity ETFs, by virtue of holding stocks, have the inherent volatility and risks of the stock market.

Bond ETFs

If you are wondering what is a bond ETF, it is simply a fund that holds fixed income instruments. You could buy one bond ETF instead of buying individual government or corporate bonds.

These funds earn revenue by receiving interest payments on the underlying bonds. They’re traded on the stock exchanges like equity ETFs so you can easily buy and sell for bond exposure without the hassle of the individual bond market.

Commodity ETFs

Commodity ETFs track the prices of physical commodities such as gold, silver, crude oil or agricultural products. These funds give investors access to commodities without the need for physical storage.

Some commodity ETFs hold the physical commodity in a vault, such as gold ETFs. Others track the commodity’s price moves with financial derivatives and futures contracts.

Leveraged ETFs

When you ask what is a leveraged ETF, you have to know that these are complex financial instruments. Leveraged ETFs use financial derivatives and debt to magnify the performance of an underlying index.

For example, a 2x leveraged ETF tries to deliver the daily return of 2x the underlying index. But this leverage cuts both ways which means losses are also amplified. Because of this high risk, leveraged ETFs are usually not recommended for beginner investors.

Inverse ETFs

Inverse ETFs are structured to move in the exact opposite direction of whatever index or asset they track. This inverse ETF will increase by 1% if the underlying index declines by 1%.

The use of derivatives in these products is the key to this reverse performance. They are typically used by savvy short-term traders looking to hedge their portfolios or speculate on market downturns. Inverse ETFs, like leveraged funds, come with special risks and require close monitoring.

Thematic ETFs

Thematic ETFs concentrate on particular long-term trends or niche sectors, rather than broad market indices. They might focus on issues like AI, clean energy, cybersecurity, or healthcare innovation.

These funds contain the stocks of companies that are actively engaged in the chosen theme. Thematic ETFs give investors exposure to new sectors, but tend to be more volatile than broad equity ETFs.

ETF Vs Mutual Fund And Index Fund: What Is The Difference?

All these three, including ETFs, mutual funds and index funds can hold baskets of assets, but they differ in their trading, pricing, costs and structure.

ETF vs Mutual Fund

The biggest difference between an ETF vs mutual fund is how they are traded. ETFs trade on the stock exchanges during the day. So you can buy and sell at different prices during the day.

Mutual funds are only priced once a day after the market closes and are based on the Net Asset Value (NAV). Furthermore, ETFs tend to have lower expense ratios than actively managed mutual funds. This difference in cost structure has been a huge driver of the explosion of global ETF assets.

ETF Vs Index Fund

People often confuse ETFs and index funds, but the ETF vs index fund distinction comes down to the fund’s legal structure. An index fund is a strategy that simply mirrors a market index.

Both mutual funds and ETFs can be index funds. The big difference is that an index ETF trades throughout the day on an exchange. An index mutual fund has the usual mutual fund structure and is traded once daily.

Minimum Investment Differences

ETFs typically have very low entry barriers. They trade like stocks so the minimum investment is usually the price of just one single share. Some brokers even let you trade fractional ETF shares.

On the other hand, mutual funds usually have a flat minimum initial investment. Depending on the mutual fund provider the minimum can be from hundreds to thousands of dollars. This makes ETFs more accessible for smaller portfolios.

Cost Differences

In fund investing, cost matters. For example, the average expense ratio for index equity ETFs fell to just 0.14%, according to the Investment Company Institute. That’s a whole lot less than most traditional mutual funds.

But when trading, ETF investors should also consider brokerage fees, commissions and the bid-ask spread. ETFs are generally cheap, but the cost can vary significantly depending on the fund, how it’s managed and the platform you use.

What Is An ETF In The Stock Market?

An ETF is a listed fund which is traded on an exchange and has a ticker symbol on the stock market.

ETFs Are Listed On Exchanges

An ETF in the stock market is officially traded on a recognized stock exchange, just like corporate stocks. There are hundreds of different ETFs listed on major exchanges like NYSE, NASDAQ, NSE and BSE.

ETFs trade on regulated exchanges, so they have to live up to the strict reporting and transparency standards. Such oversight is useful in ensuring that the fund accurately reflects its underlying assets.

ETF Ticker Symbols

Like stocks, each ETF has its own ticker symbol. The symbol is a short string of letters that identifies the fund on trading platforms.

For instance, the popular SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust trades under the ticker symbol SPY. To research or trade an ETF, simply type its ticker symbol into your broker’s search bar.

ETF Trading Hours

Like stocks, ETFs are traded on exchanges and thus have the same trading hours as the exchange they are listed on. The exchange opens at 9:30 AM and closes at 4:00 PM, so you can trade the ETF during the same time frame.

The price of the ETF is constantly moving during these hours as buyers and sellers interact. That’s a big difference from mutual funds, which only process trades after the market has fully closed.

How ETF Orders Work

You can use the same order types when buying and selling ETFs as you can with regular stocks. You can place a market order to buy the ETF at the best price you can find right now.

Or you can set a limit order where you specify the maximum price you are prepared to pay exactly. You will also see the bid-ask spread, the slim margin between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay and the lowest price a seller will accept. ETFs with high liquidity tend to have very tight spreads.

Are ETFs Worth It?

ETFs can be useful for many investors, as they can provide diversification, low cost, flexibility and transparency, but they still carry risk.

Potential Benefits Of ETFs

An EY ETF report found that global ETF assets reached record highs of over $11.6 trillion, for good reason. The main benefit is immediate diversification. By purchasing one ETF, you are exposed to dozens or hundreds of assets, which spreads the risk of any single company failing.

ETFs tend to have lower expense ratios than actively managed mutual funds. They are traded on the exchange and hence you can trade them during the day. They are transparent in structure and you can see what the fund is invested in on any given day.

Important Limitations

Even with the benefits, ETFs have important limitations. Being fully exposed to market risk, should the underlying index fall, the value of the ETF will fall with it.

Tracking error is another issue that some ETFs experience, the difference between the actual return of the ETF and the index that it is tracking. In addition, there are very specific and niche ETFs that might also have lower liquidity, which can result in a wider spread and higher trading costs.

Who ETFs May Suit

As for whether or not ETFs are worthwhile, it will depend on you and your financial plan. For investors seeking an easy, low-cost, passive way to grow wealth over time, broad market index ETFs may be an excellent option.

More complex products, such as leveraged, inverse and very thematic ETFs, are ones that are advisable only for active, experienced traders. These are sophisticated ETFs and you must be careful and pay close attention to them on a daily basis, and know the risks of derivatives.

What Are Examples Of Popular ETFs?

The most popular ETFs are broad market trackers like SPY and QQQ. But there are also specialized funds that focus on commodities, bonds, and emerging themes.

QQQ ETF

The Invesco QQQ Trust is one of the most traded ETFs in the world. For those who don’t know what is QQQ ETF, it is tracking the Nasdaq-100 Index.

That is, it comprises 100 of the biggest non-financial corporations traded on the Nasdaq exchange. Due to its particular criteria, QQQ is heavily skewed toward large technology and communications companies. It’s a popular choice for investors looking for broad exposure to the tech sector.

SPY ETF

The most well-known and oldest ETF in existence is the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust, better known by its ticker symbol, SPY. SPY is an ETF that follows the S&P 500 Index, which is comprised of the stocks of 500 of the largest publicly traded companies in the United States

Many investors use SPY as a cornerstone for their long-term equity portfolios because of its broad market representation.

Gold ETFs

Gold ETFs provide investors with direct access to the price movements of gold. And that type is exemplified by the SPDR Gold Shares (GLD).

Instead of buying physical gold bars, storing them and insuring them, you just buy shares of the ETF. The fund itself holds physical gold bullion in secure vaults and the share price closely tracks the spot price of gold.

AI ETFs

With the rapid development of technology, the popularity of thematic funds on artificial intelligence has soared. When you take part in an AI ETF investing, you are buying into a fund that owns shares of companies that develop AI hardware, software and services.

Some examples are the Global X Robotics and Artificial Intelligence ETF (BOTZ). These thematic funds let investors concentrate on a high-growth area without having to choose the one winning AI firm.

Bond ETFs

Bond ETFs provide a simple way to buy fixed-income debt. The Vanguard Total Bond Market ETF (BND) is a common example.

This fund tracks a broad, market-weighted index of U.S. investment-grade bonds, including government debt and corporate debt. Bond ETFs are often used by investors to counteract the greater volatility of their equity ETF holdings.

How To Invest In ETFs

You’ll need to open a brokerage account to buy ETFs, research your fund options, check costs, place a trade and monitor your investment.

Step 1: Open A Brokerage Account

To learn how to invest in ETFs, you first need to open an account with a registered stockbroker or trading platform. Most online brokers today have access to a wide variety of ETFs.

Choose a platform that gives you access to the particular exchanges where the ETFs you want are listed. Look for platforms that have transparent fee structures and reliable trading tools.

Step 2: Research And Choose An ETF

Don’t buy an ETF just because it’s popular or has a good name. Read about the fund’s goal, the index it tracks and its biggest investments.

Read the fund’s prospectus so you know precisely what you are buying. Make sure the ETF fits your own risk tolerance, time horizon and overall investment strategy.

Step 3: Check The Expense Ratio And Tracking Error

Always invest your capital in the ETF after checking the expense ratio. The fund provider will automatically debit this annual fee. ETF fees are usually low, but there is a big difference in price between broad passive funds and specialised active funds.

Also, look at the fund’s tracking error to see how well it has historically tracked the actual index it purports to track.

Step 4: Place A Buy Order

When you choose an ETF, you execute the trade on your broker platform. Search the fund’s unique ticker symbol.

Choose a market order to execute immediately or a limit order to define the exact price you’re willing to pay. Once you confirm the order during open market hours, you will get the shares to your account.

Step 5: Monitor And Review

Investing is not a “set and forget” process. Periodically review your ETF holdings to ensure they are still in line with your financial goals.

If the fund’s strategy has changed, or the expense ratio has increased, or your overall portfolio has drifted from its intended asset allocation, check to see. Re-balance your holdings as needed to maintain your desired risk level.

What Are The Key Risks Of ETF Investing?

The main risks of investing in ETFs are market risk, tracking error, liquidity risk, currency risk and risks of leveraged products.

Market Risk

The greatest risk in ETF investing is simply market risk. An ETF is just a container for underlying assets. It doesn’t protect you from market declines.

If you own an S&P 500 ETF and the broader stock market goes down, the value of your ETF will go down with it. Your capital is at risk and the value of investments can fall as well as rise.

Tracking Error

Tracking error is the difference in performance between an ETF and the index it is tracking. This discrepancy may be caused by internal management fees, transaction costs or cash drag within the fund.

Good broad-market ETFs tend to have very low tracking error, but smaller or more complicated funds can drift quite a bit from their benchmarks over time.

Liquidity Risk

For example, large ETFs like SPY trade millions of shares a day, but smaller or newer ETFs can suffer from low trading volume. This creates a liquidity risk.

Less liquidity means a wider bid-ask spread (more expensive to get in and out) . In extreme situations, during periods of market stress, you may find it difficult to sell your shares quickly at a fair market price.

Leveraged ETF Risk

Leveraged and inverse ETFs carry unique and amplified risks. These funds re-set their leverage daily. Over periods longer than a day , the effect of these daily resets can become so pronounced that the returns of the ETF can diverge entirely from the long-term return of the index.

Mathematically, this decay means leveraged ETFs can lose a lot of money in volatile markets and are not meant to be held long term.

Currency Risk

When you buy an international ETF that holds assets in foreign currencies, you’re exposed to currency risk.

Changes in the value of the foreign currency relative to your home currency can affect your returns. If the foreign stocks do well, the gains can be wiped out by a stronger home currency when the ETF’s NAV is calculated.

What Common Mistakes Should Beginners Avoid With ETFs?

Beginners should avoid chasing past performance, ignoring the underlying holdings, overtrading and using complex leveraged funds without experience.

Chasing Recent Performance

Picking an ETF because of its spectacular return last year is a classic beginner mistake. Markets go through cycles and the best performing sector one year is often the worst the next year.

Focus on building a diverse, sustainable portfolio instead of chasing hot trends. Always look at the expense ratio before you buy. High fees will quietly eat away your returns, regardless of how well the fund has done in the past.

Using Leveraged ETFs Without Understanding

Many beginners are attracted to 2x or 3x leveraged ETFs because they promise quick profits. They don’t understand how mathematically resetting daily leverage erodes value in volatile, sideways markets.

Using these complex derivative products often results in heavy capital loss due to a lack of a clear short-term trading plan and strict risk controls.

Not Checking Underlying Holdings

Never buy an ETF just for its name. A “Clean Energy” ETF and a “Green Tech” ETF might seem similar, but they are full of very different companies.

Always check under the hood. You should go to the fund provider’s website and look at the top 10 holdings to see which exact companies and sectors are driving the fund’s performance.

ETFs are confused with single stocks

ETFs are traded on exchanges like single stocks, but they function differently. One stock can soar on an earnings report or plummet on a scandal.

Broad ETFs are slower because the rest of the basket averages out the extreme movements of individual companies. Don’t look for single-stock volatility and overnight riches in a broad index ETF.

Confusing ETFs With Single Stocks

Because ETFs are so easy to trade intraday, beginners are often tempted to constantly jump in and out of the market. This overtrading results in unnecessary costs in the form of brokerage fees and bid-ask spreads.

ETFs are not best used by most investors for quick day trading but rather for long-term buy and hold.

FAQs

An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is an investment fund that trades on a public stock exchange like a regular company share. The fund holds a basket of assets such as stocks, bonds, or commodities.

The main difference is the flexibility in trading. Just like stocks, ETFs are traded on stock exchanges and their prices change throughout the day. Mutual Funds are valued only once in a day at NAV. ETFs also tend to have lower expense ratios.

A leveraged ETF is a fund that uses financial derivatives and debt to amplify the returns of an index (such as 2x or 3x). These are high-risk instruments as they also magnify losses and are subject to mathematical decay over time.

A bond ETF is an exchange-traded fund that only holds fixed-income securities such as government or corporate bonds. It gives investors diversified bond exposure and a steady income stream without the hassle of buying individual bonds.

To invest in an ETF, you have to open a brokerage account. Then you have to research funds that fit your investment strategy, look at their expense ratios and place a buy order during market hours with the ticker symbol for the fund. Remember, all investments involve risk.

Conclusion

The first step to flexible, diversified investing is understanding what an ETF is.

Exchange-traded funds have transformed the financial world by making it as easy for the average investor to buy a wide basket of assets as it is to buy a single stock.

ETFs are a transparent and often low-cost vehicle, whether you want to track the S&P 500, buy fixed-income bonds or invest in thematic sectors like artificial intelligence.

But the value of an ETF is always changing with the market. They carry inherent risks and your capital is not guaranteed. Always check the expense ratios, research the underlying holdings, and ensure the fund fits your long-term financial goals before you commit your funds.
