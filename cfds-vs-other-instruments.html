CFD vs Futures: How CFDs Compare to Other Instruments

Compare CFDs vs futures, stocks, ETFs, forex, and options. Learn the key differences in ownership, leverage, costs, and expiry to pick the right one for you.

STARTRADER Research DeskPublished 21 June 2026 · Updated 21 August 2026 · 27 min read
Link copied

The major difference between CFDs and other financial instruments lies in the problem of asset ownership and the flexibility of the contract.

It’s not unusual to see futures, stocks, ETFs, options, and forex products all in the same space. When you’re just beginning, it can be hard to decide which to trade.

Here’s something to think about: If you can get exposure to crude oil or the S&P 500 with either of two instruments, does it really matter which instrument you use? Why do so many traders compare a CFD vs. a futures contract? The answer comes down to structure, cost, contract terms and what rights, if any you actually get.

This guide is geared towards beginner and intermediate traders who want a clear factual comparison of CFDs versus other common financial instruments. By the end, you will understand the key structural differences, typical costs and risk considerations that distinguish each product.

Quick answer

The main difference between CFDs and futures is that most CFDs do not have a fixed expiry date, whereas futures contracts always do.

Futures are traded on an exchange and have standardized contract sizes. CFDs are traded over the counter (OTC) through a broker and offer more flexible sizing. Both use leverage, which means both are high risk of rapid loss.

What Is The Difference Between CFDs And Other Instruments?

Unlike other financial instruments, CFDs let traders follow price movements without ever owning the underlying asset.

Most traditional instruments require you to purchase and own a physical asset or a share of a company. Derivatives such as CFDs simply track the live price of that asset. It’s a fundamental structural difference that changes the way you interact with the market.

Knowing the difference helps you to understand costs, tax implications and trading strategies. This is where the comparison of CFDs with traditional stocks or complex derivatives originates.

What Is A CFD?

A Contract for Difference or CFD in short is a derivative of an underlying asset price that enables traders to make or lose money when the price changes without actually owning the asset.

When you open a CFD you are agreeing with your broker to exchange the difference between the opening and closing price hence the name. CFDs are offered on shares, indices, forex, commodities, ETFs and bonds. One of the reasons CFDs are so popular with retail traders across the globe is that

Why Compare CFDs To Other Instruments?

By comparing instruments, traders can see what they’re getting and giving up before they put their capital on the line. You can get exposure to the price of a share via a share CFD but you do not get any shareholder rights.

Unlike a futures contract, a CFD trader usually does not have to deal with a mandatory expiry date. These structural differences affect costs, risks and the behavior of a product over time.

Key Characteristics Of CFDs

In CFDs, you do not own the asset. You get instant access to various markets, can trade in both directions, and enjoy high leverage. You never own the underlying asset so you don’t have the logistical headaches like having to pay for commodity storage.

CFDs also allow you to access multiple markets from a single platform, such as MT4 or MT5. The ability to go long or short with ease gives you the ultimate flexibility to respond to any market condition. Plus, leverage means you only need a small amount of the total value of the trade to get started.

How Do CFDs Differ From Futures?

The main differences between CFDs and futures are the standardization of contracts, the location they are traded, and inflexible expiration dates.

When you consider these two derivatives, execution mechanics are everything. Futures are structured, regulated and rigid.

CFDs are highly flexible, and this is why they appeal so much to retail traders. They are both insanely powerful tools but they serve slightly different crowds. If you want to know more about these core mechanics, you can check the CFD vs futures key differences.

What Is A Futures Contract?

A futures contract is a legally binding contract to buy or sell a specific asset at a fixed price on a future date.

A futures contract is, at its core, a price-locking feature, originally designed as a hedge for physical producers and buyers against price volatility. They are widely used today by speculators.

These are standardized contracts traded on exchanges. Futures contracts specify a certain quantity of the asset and a specific expiration date. Customization is impossible.

CFD Vs Futures: Core Differences

Futures are very standardized with exact dates of expiration while CFDs are flexible with sizes of contracts and do not have a fixed expiration .

Futures are traded on public exchanges and that centralizes pricing. CFDs are traded over the counter (OTC) via a broker and it is the broker that provides the liquidity and pricing.

If you hold your position open after the daily cut-off, you may be charged overnight financing when trading CFDs. Futures do not have these daily financing charges but the cost of carry is typically baked into the futures price itself.

Contract Size And Flexibility

Futures are bought and sold in large, fixed increments, while you can buy or sell parts of a contract with a CFD.

Fractional sizing allows you to tailor your CFD trade to your account size and risk management rules. If the standard contract is too big, trade a micro or mini CFD.

Standardized futures contracts are rigid to a fault. If one oil futures contract is 1,000 barrels, you have to trade in multiples of 1,000. This usually requires a lot more capital than CFDs.

Margin And Leverage Differences

Both instruments also use leverage, but while futures spreads are set by centralized exchanges, CFD spreads are determined by the broker.

The mechanics of leverage in both products are essentially the same: You post a deposit to control a larger position. However, futures exchanges have initial margin and maintenance margin rules that depend on the overall volatility of the market.

Margin requirements for CFDs are usually determined by the broker as a percentage of the total value of the trade. This way beginners often find it easier to understand CFD margin calculations.

Counterparty Risk

CFDs carry broker exposure, while futures rely on a central clearing house to secure trades.

Counterparty risk is the risk that the other side of your trade does not pay. CFDs are traded against your broker as counterparty so it is important to choose a well regulated platform like STARTRADER to keep your capital safe.

Futures use a clearinghouse structure. The exchange is the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer . The risk of a single defaulting counterparty is virtually eliminated.

Who Typically Uses CFDs And Futures?

CFDs are very popular among retail traders, while futures have high volumes from institutional traders and commercial hedgers.

Retail traders favor CFDs because of their lower barriers to entry, flexible trade sizes and user-friendly platforms. There’s no expiry date, so swing trades are easier to manage over a few days.

Futures are popular with institutional players, hedgers and large speculators. They want the deep liquidity of centralized exchanges, and the ability to take physical possession of assets if necessary.

How Do CFDs Compare To Stocks And Shares?

When you trade CFDs on shares you are exposed to price movements but do not actually own the company.

Traditional stock trading is the backbone of the financial markets. When people think of Wall Street they usually think of buying and selling shares.

It is interesting to look at the CFD vs stock dynamics to understand the gap between derivatives and physical equities. Whether you go with one or the other will dictate your trading time frame and capital needs.

What Happens When You Buy Shares?

When you buy shares, you are buying a small piece of actual ownership in a company that trades publicly.

Purchasing physical shares makes you a shareholder. Your goal is typically long-term capital appreciation, with the possibility of income from dividends.

This traditional method is the pre-payment of the full value of the shares. It’s a simple process but it does require a lot of money tied up.

CFD Vs Stock Ownership

A CFD on a stock just reflects the price of the stock so you technically never own a piece of the business.

Trading CFDs bypasses the delays in settlement and traditional stock exchange fees because there is no ownership transfer. You can move in and out of positions almost instantly.

If you want to learn more about how these mechanics work in everyday trading, see the differences between CFD vs share trading.

Voting Rights And Shareholder Benefits

Physical shareholders have voting rights on company issues, a benefit that is totally missing in CFD trading.

Shareholders vote on board members , corporate actions and mergers . Annual reports are sent to them and they are invited to shareholder meetings.

CFD traders miss out on all of these corporate benefits. Your sole connection is to the price of the underlying equity.

Dividend Treatment In CFDs

Dividends are simulated by CFD providers through a cash adjustment to your trading account.

As an example, if you are long a CFD on a dividend paying stock, your account will be credited with an amount equal to the dividend. This way you don’t miss out on the financial benefit of the payout.

On the other hand, if you have a short CFD position on that same stock, the amount of the dividend will be deducted from your account. This is like the mechanics of short selling in the physical stock market.

Leverage And Capital Requirements

You only need to put down a small part of the total trade value for CFDs, and the usual requirement to buy shares is the full capital commitment.

Leverage allows a trader to open a large position in a blue-chip stock with a relatively small account balance. That is why many active players prefer CFDs for efficiency.

Traditional shares can sometimes be traded on margin but the leverage ratios are usually much lower compared to those offered by CFDs. You can compare these capital requirements further in our guide to CFDs vs shares.

How Do CFDs Compare To ETFs?

CFDs are designed to be used for leveraged short term speculation whereas ETFs are used traditionally for long term unleveraged diversification.

What Is An ETF?

An ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) is an investment fund that is traded on stock exchanges and contains many underlying assets such as stocks, commodities, or bonds.

ETFs are meant to follow the performance of a specific index or sector. If you buy just one share of an ETF, you get fractional ownership of dozens or hundreds of companies.

This structure gives instant diversification. “It’s a very efficient way to invest in broad macroeconomic trends rather than trying to pick individual winning stocks,” he said.

Ownership Differences

If you buy an ETF, you own a unit of the fund. If you trade an ETF CFD, you’re just trading the market price of the fund.

If you own a unit of an ETF, you own a real asset that can be held in a traditional brokerage or retirement account for decades.

An ETF CFD does not mean you own the fund’s underlying basket of assets. You’re just trading the volatility of the market price of the ETF on the open market.

Diversification And Market Exposure

ETFs are diversified by design, and trading CFDs on those ETFs allows you to speculate on the movement of entire sectors at once.

An ETF automatically spreads risk over multiple assets. If one company in the ETF performs poorly, the other companies in the fund help cushion the blow.

Trading a CFD on an ETF gives you that same broad exposure, but with the added advantage of being able to easily short the sector if you think the overall market is going down.

Leverage And Trading Flexibility

CFDs provide a lot of leverage on ETF prices, so they are good for aggressive trades over a short period of time.

Standard ETFs are traded without leverage, but there are some specialized leveraged ETFs. You have to purchase each unit at the full price.

You can use ETF CFDs to add margin to these diversified assets. This increases your buying power, but you have to be careful because the risk of quick losses is higher.

Typical Holding Periods

ETFs are usually long term ‘buy and hold’ investments. CFDs have overnight fees which makes them expensive to hold long term.

ETFs are often held by investors for years, and pay just a small annual management fee. They’re fantastic tools for retirement planning and slow wealth build-up.

CFDs are subject to daily financing costs. Holding an ETF CFD for months can see these overnight fees adding up and eating into your profits, so they’re more suitable for day trading or short swing trades.

How Do CFDs Compare To Forex?

CFDs are not mutually exclusive to forex trading and in fact the vast majority of retail forex is traded almost exclusively through CFD contracts.

When traders compare the two, they are usually comparing the asset class (currencies) with the derivative wrapper (CFDs).

To clear up this overlap, it is helpful to see how the mechanics of CFD vs forex structures work to see how currency pairs are executed on modern trading platforms.

Understanding The Relationship Between CFDs And Forex

CFDs are the vehicle through which retail traders have access to the massive, decentralized foreign exchange market.

Spot forex is the currency traded by large financial institutions on the interbank market. Retail traders can not directly trade this market as the amount of capital needed is large.

To fill this gap brokers offer CFDs that reflect the spot forex prices. If you are trading a currency pair on a retail platform you are trading a forex CFD.

Forex CFDs Explained

Forex CFDs are contracts based on the exchange rate of two currencies, allowing you to speculate on the relative strength of the pair.

The EUR/USD pair is a bet on the Euro going up or down against the US Dollar.

The CFD contract just pays out the difference in the exchange rate between opening and closing the trade, based on the broker’s liquidity.

Market Access And Asset Variety

Pure forex brokers only offer currency pairs, while CFD providers offer forex with stocks, commodities and indices.

The main advantage of trading through a CFD platform is that it brings all your trades under one roof. No need for one account for currencies and another one for equities.

Switching from GBPJPY to gold or the S&P 500 is seamless, from the same margin pool, and from the same screen.

Margin And Leverage Considerations

Forex CFDs are usually the most leveraged instruments available on the retail market, because of the high liquidity of the major currency pairs.

Major currencies such as the USD, EUR, and JPY are very stable and liquid. This stability allows brokers to provide substantial leverage on these specific CFDs.

Forex CFD margin requirements are generally lower than stock and commodity CFD margin requirements. However, the high leverage in forex can still lead to quick losses if the market moves against you.

When Traders Use Forex CFDs

Traders also use CFDs on forex to speculate on short-term movements caused by the release of macroeconomic data and statements from central banks.

Forex CFDs are very popular with day traders because currencies are traded 24 hours a day, 5 days a week.

They allow traders to react continuously to global news, such as interest rate decisions or employment reports, without the limitations of regular exchange opening hours.

How Do CFDs Compare To Equity?

Comparing CFDs to equities is essentially comparing leveraged speculation on prices to actual ownership of a company.

Equity is a residual claim on the assets of the firm. CFDs are a contract between you and a broker.

For a quick overview of the interplay between these two concepts, you can study the CFD vs equity comparison.

What Is Equity?

Equity is the ownership interest that a person or entity has in an asset or a business.

If you are an owner of equity in a public company, your wealth increases when the company performs well. It’s a bedrock of traditional finance.

Equity holders are the real owners of the business and bear the ultimate risk but also enjoy the ultimate rewards of long-term corporate growth.

Ownership Vs Price Exposure

An equity gives a real ownership right. A CFD only gives a financial exposure to the market value of the equity.

That’s the recurring theme in trading derivatives. Price exposure allows you to play the movement of the asset without the legal and logistical attachments of ownership.

If a company does bankrupt, equity holders are usually wiped out last. If the underlying asset falls in value the holders of CFDs will simply see the value of their derivative contract fall.

Long And Short Trading Opportunities

CFDs allow short selling of equity assets in a simple manner, whereas with physical shares this is often complicated and restricted.

To short physical equity, you need a margin account and to borrow shares from your broker, which can be subject to strict rules and borrowing fees.

CFDs eliminate these hurdles. You just click “sell ” on the trading platform. This makes CFDs the instrument of choice for traders who want to profit from falling equity markets.

Equity CFDs In Practice

Equity CFDs are used by traders to hedge their physical stock portfolios or day-trade earnings announcements aggressively.

If you own physical shares in a company, but expect a decrease in the price in the near future, you can take out a short CFD position on the shares to hedge against possible losses without needing to sell your actual equity.

Active traders also use equity CFDs to profit from the huge price movements that can accompany quarterly earnings reports, taking advantage of the short-term volatility.

How Do CFDs Compare To Options?

Options let you trade an asset at a set price, while CFDs create an immediate obligation to settle the price difference.

Both are derivatives but are based on totally different mathematical and structural principles. CFDs are pretty straightforward. Options are very complex. You can explore the difference between CFDs and options to navigate these two different derivatives.

What Are Options?

Options are derivative contracts that allow a buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a specified strike price before an expiration date.

Call options give you the right to buy and put options give you the right to sell a security. To get this right you pay an up-front fee, called a premium. At most, if the market moves against you, you can lose the premium you paid.

Rights Versus Obligations

Options give the holder the right to execute the trade, whereas CFDs are a firm obligation to exchange the full difference in price on closing.

This is the essence of the difference. When an option expires out of the money, it just doesn’t have any value. You lose your premium but no more than that. With a CFD you’re stuck in the trade until you close it, and you’re liable for the entire price difference.

Expiry Dates And Time Decay

Time decay is a big factor for options as they draw closer to their fixed expiry date, but most CFDs have no hard expiry.

An option loses value every single day. This is called Theta. Even if the underlying asset moves in your favor, the option may lose value if it doesn’t move fast enough. CFDs are not subject to time decay. The CFD is priced the same as the live price of the underlying asset.

Cost Structures Compared

Premium payment up front is involved in options, while costs are generally based on the bid-ask spread and overnight financing in CFDs.

The value of an option premium is determined by a complex formula, which includes volatility, time to expiry and the price of the underlying asset. CFD pricing is far more transparent. You pay the spread when you open the trade and a financing charge if you hold it overnight.

Complexity And Learning Curve

Options trading is not as simple as CFD trading, as there are a number of variables, known as ‘the Greeks’, that need to be managed.

To be successful trading options you must know Delta, Gamma, Theta and Vega. You are predicting volatility and time, not just price direction. CFDs are only directional. If you think the price will rise, you buy. This simplicity is why CFDs are generally considered to be easier to access.

Are CFDs The Same As Investing?

CFDs are not investments, but rather are speculative derivative products used for short term trading and not for long term wealth accumulation.

The terminology is often confused but the distinction is important for financial planning. Investing and trading are different mindsets and risk management.

If you want to get a better understanding of how the two approaches differ, check out the principles of CFD vs investing.

What Is Traditional Investing?

In traditional investing, you buy an asset and plan to hold onto it for years, to benefit from slow and steady growth in the market.

Investors look at the fundamentals. They buy stocks, bonds or real estate for intrinsic value and compounding returns over time.

The goal is usually to slowly maintain and build wealth, accepting short term market fluctuations.

How CFD Trading Differs From Investing

In CFD trading it’s all about trying to make money from short-term price movements with leverage and you don’t care about the long-term fundamentals of the asset at all.

Traders do not want to build a portfolio. They look to extract profit from daily or weekly volatility.

CFDs are leveraged, so the risk profile is dramatically higher than buying an index fund in a retirement account.

Ownership And Wealth Building

Traditional investing builds real wealth through ownership of assets. CFDs generate localized profits or losses based on market timing.

Over time stocks tend to go up in value and pay dividends directly increasing your net worth.

The wealth created by CFDs does not compound in the same way. Every trade is an individual speculative event to seize immediate cash.

Leverage Risk And Short-Term Trading

CFDs contain built-in leverage, so they’re not suited to “buy and hold” strategies, because your capital could quickly deplete.

If a traditional non-leveraged stock falls 10%, you just wait for it to rebound.

If a leveraged CFD falls 10% the losses are magnified and can eat through your entire trading account, meaning you get a margin call before the asset has a chance to recover.

Common Misconceptions About CFD Investing

It is a dangerous misconception that you can use CFDs to ‘invest’ in companies over the long-term, ignoring the crippling effect of overnight financing fees.

If you hold a CFD position for months, you will pay massive financing charges.

These fees can quickly erode any potential profit from price appreciation in the asset. CFDs are a tactical tool, not a long term investment.

What Instruments Can You Trade As CFDs?

CFDs offer a flexible wrapper to allow you to trade on price movements in almost any major global asset class.

The key draw for active traders is its versatility. You are not restricted to a single type of market and so you can adapt to any macro-economic environment.

From safe-haven assets to the most volatile tech shares, understanding the scope of available markets is key.

This is the sector-wide diversification of an ETF combined with the short-term speculative power of a CFD.

  • Stock CFDs: follow the share price of individual companies on stock exchanges around the world.
  • Forex CFDs: track the price movements of currency pairs. Major, minor and exotic.
  • Commodity CFDs: these are raw materials like gold, silver, crude oil and natural gas.
  • Index CFDs: track key market indices like the S&P 500, FTSE 100 and DAX 40.
  • ETF CFDs: provide leveraged exposure to the price movements of ETFs.
  • Bond CFDs: track prices of government or corporate bonds, used for interest rate exposure.

Each asset class has its own behavior, typical volatility, liquidity profile and cost structure. STARTRADER’s guide to CFD bonds provides the mechanics and key considerations to gain an in-depth understanding of the mechanics of fixed income instruments as derivatives.

What Should Traders Consider When Choosing An Instrument?

When comparing CFDs and futures, you need to critically evaluate your trading goals, your risk tolerance and the specific costs associated with your strategy.

Never choose an instrument based on popularity. The mechanics of the instrument have to be adapted to your duration of trade and capital risk.

Trading Goals

Is your main objective short term speculation on the direction of price or long term growth and preservation of wealth? If you want to day trade volatile assets, CFDs give you the flexibility you need. Physical ETFs or equity shares are the right vehicle for a retirement portfolio.

Risk Tolerance

Evaluate your capacity to engage in leveraged risk. Leverage increases your exposure. You need to be able to absorb a sudden margin call emotionally and financially. If you can’t, avoid highly leveraged derivatives and stay in a forgiving, unleveraged environment .

Cost Structure

Consider the effect of spreads, commissions and overnight financing charges. If you are looking to swing trade for several weeks then the daily financing charges of a CFD will eat into your profits and a flat commission futures contract may be cheaper. Always work out your break even point.

Complexity And Learning Curve

Choose an instrument that suits your experience level. CFDs are directional so they are easier for a beginner to understand. Options trading, while beneficial, can be daunting to new traders with its complexities, such as time decay, leading to unnecessary losses.

Conclusion

CFDs (contracts for difference) are a very flexible type of derivative that provides access to global markets without the heavy logistical baggage of owning the physical asset itself.

The CFDs vs futures debate is never about which instrument is objectively better. It’s all about finding the right instrument that fits your trading goals, your capital size and your time frame. Futures provide deep liquidity and central clearing for larger players, while CFDs offer fractional sizing, mechanical simplicity and instant multi-asset access directly from platforms such as MT4 and MT5.
