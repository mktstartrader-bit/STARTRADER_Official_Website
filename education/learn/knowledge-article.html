Why invest in bonds? A complete guide to bond investing for beginners

Stocks get the conversation; bonds get the bigger market. What a bond actually is, the main types, how yield works, what interest rates do to prices, and the ways to buy in.

STARTRADER Research DeskPublished 3 August 2026 · 12 min read
Link copied

Stocks are the main topic of most investing conversations. Bonds seldom are — which is odd, because the bond market is larger by total value than the equity market. Governments and companies have been raising money by selling bonds for centuries.

Anyone building a rounded portfolio should understand what bonds are for. Unlike equities they offer regular income and a degree of price stability, which can buffer a stock-heavy portfolio. They are not risk-free — that is one of investing’s most common misconceptions — but they do a different job from stocks, and that is precisely why they are useful.

Quick answer

Bonds pay a steady stream of income as fixed coupon payments and can stabilise a portfolio alongside stocks. They are usually less risky than equities, but they carry credit risk (the issuer may fail) and interest rate risk (values fall when rates rise). Bond values move both ways; they are not guaranteed. This is not investment advice.

What is a bond?

A bond is a debt instrument — a loan from an investor to a government or a company that pays a fixed amount of interest for a set period. When you buy the bond you are lending; the issuer agrees to pay a fixed coupon at regular intervals and to return the principal at maturity.

Key bond terms

Key bond terms and their definitions
TermDefinition
Principal (face value)The original amount lent to the issuer, returned at maturity.
Coupon rateThe annual interest rate paid on the bond’s face value.
Maturity dateThe date on which the issuer repays the principal.
Coupon paymentThe regular interest payment made to the bondholder.
Secondary marketWhere bonds change hands between investors before maturity.
Credit ratingA score reflecting how likely the issuer is to repay.

A bond can be held to maturity, collecting coupons along the way and the principal at the end, or bought and sold on the secondary market beforehand. On that secondary market prices move with interest rates, credit conditions and sentiment — and that is where most of the risk and most of the opportunity live.

Why invest in bonds?

Investors hold bonds for three main reasons: regular income, diversification and capital preservation. The case is not that bonds beat equities over the long run — historically they have not. The case is that they behave differently, and in a well-built portfolio that is worth having.

What each benefit means, and what to weigh against it
BenefitWhat it meansConsideration
Regular incomeCoupon payments provide a predictable cash flow.The coupon is fixed, so its real value erodes if inflation rises.
Portfolio diversificationBonds often move differently from equities.Correlation can rise in a crisis, when you need it least.
Capital preservationGovernment bonds are generally lower risk than stocks.Prices still move with interest rates.
Inflation protectionInflation-linked bonds adjust payments with inflation.Standard bonds offer no inflation protection at all.

The coupon is the heart of it. Unlike a dividend, which a company can cut at will, a coupon is set in advance: a 4% coupon on $100,000 of face value pays $4,000 a year, split into semi-annual or quarterly payments, whatever the market is doing. That predictability has real value to anyone with cash-flow needs.

Bonds and equities react differently to the same economy. In slow growth, government bonds often rally while equities fall — imperfectly, and never guaranteed, but often enough to matter.

Standard bonds pay a fixed coupon, so if inflation runs above the coupon rate the purchasing power of that income shrinks. Inflation-linked bonds — TIPS in the United States, index-linked gilts in the United Kingdom — adjust both principal and coupon with inflation, which suits long horizons.

Types of bonds: government, corporate and high-yield

Bonds are not one risk level. Knowing where a bond sits on the spectrum matters more than the headline yield.

Government bondsSovereign governmentsLow to moderateLowerConservative investors seeking stability
Treasury billsGovernment, short-termVery lowLowestCash management and capital preservation
Corporate bondsCompanies, investment gradeModerateHigherInvestors wanting more than a government yield

Government bonds and treasury bills

Governments issue bonds to fund public spending, backed by the ability to tax and, in some cases, to print. US Treasuries, UK gilts, German bunds and Indian G-Secs are the large markets. Risk is country-specific: developed, stable economies default very rarely; fragile ones carry real risk. Treasury bills are the short end — weeks to a year, no coupon, sold at a discount and redeemed at face value, with the difference being the return.

Corporate and high-yield bonds

A company issuing debt pays more than a comparable government because it might not repay. Investment-grade corporate bonds offer a premium over governments at relatively low default risk. High-yield — or junk — bonds come from sub-investment-grade issuers; the extra yield is payment for the extra chance of trouble, and they need real credit analysis and active monitoring.

How bond investing works

A bond returns value two ways: the interest received while you hold it, and the difference between what you paid and what you get back.

Bonds are issued at face value, but they trade at market value. Below face value is a discount; above it is a premium. Buy at a discount and hold to maturity and you collect the coupons plus a capital gain. Buy at a premium and you take a capital loss at maturity, having received more coupon income along the way.

Holding to maturity gives certainty: a known income and a known principal on a known date, unless the issuer defaults. Selling early adds price risk, because the market value will have moved with rates and credit conditions in the meantime.

A simple example

An illustrative bond and what it pays
Face value$100,000
Coupon rate5% per annum
Annual income$5,000
Maturity10 years
Total income over the term$50,000 before taxes and fees
Principal returned$100,000 at maturity

Illustrative figures only. Actual returns depend on the purchase price, tax treatment and holding the bond to maturity.

What bond yield is, and how it is calculated

Yield is the return from a bond as a percentage, and it moves with the secondary-market price. Coupon yield is the simple one: the annual coupon over face value. Yield to maturity is the useful one, because it accounts for what you actually paid and any gain or loss against face value. Buy at a discount and yield to maturity exceeds the coupon rate; buy at a premium and it falls below it.

How interest rates affect bond prices

A bond’s coupon is fixed, so when newly issued bonds pay more, an existing bond must get cheaper for its yield to compete. That is the whole of the inverse relationship. Duration measures how sensitive a given bond is to it: the longer the time to maturity, the further the price has to travel for the same change in rates.

How to invest in bonds: funds, ETFs and direct purchase

Buying directly gives you a maturity date and a known schedule of payments, but it takes size to diversify properly. Bond mutual funds pool holdings and are professionally managed, at the cost of an annual fee and of the maturity date — a fund never matures. Bond ETFs trade like shares, usually at lower cost, with the same trade-off: continuous pricing, no maturity, no guaranteed return of principal on a date.

How to trade bonds using CFDs

A bond CFD tracks the price of a government bond future without owning the underlying. It allows a position in either direction and it is traded on margin, which magnifies both the gain and the loss. It is a trading instrument, not an income one: there is no coupon and no principal returned, so it answers a different question from buying a bond.

Leverage cuts both ways

Margin means a small price move produces a large change in your position’s value, in either direction. Size positions on what you can afford to lose, not on what the margin allows.

The key risks of bond investing

  • Interest rate riskPrices fall when rates rise, and longer maturities fall further.
  • Credit riskThe issuer may miss a coupon or fail to return the principal.
  • Inflation riskA fixed coupon buys less each year that inflation outruns it.

Common mistakes to avoid

Three recur. Treating all bonds as equally safe, when a sub-investment-grade corporate bond and a short-dated treasury bill share little but the name. Chasing a high yield without asking what the credit risk behind it is. And confusing a bond fund with owning a bond — the fund has no maturity date, so the certainty you were buying is not there.

Frequently asked questions

Are bonds risk-free?
No. That is one of the most common misconceptions in investing. Bonds carry credit risk — the issuer may fail to pay — and interest rate risk, because a bond’s market value falls when rates rise. Prices move both ways.
What is the difference between coupon yield and yield to maturity?
Coupon yield is the annual coupon divided by face value. Yield to maturity also accounts for the price you actually paid and any gain or loss against face value, so it is the more useful figure when buying on the secondary market.
Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?
A bond’s coupon is fixed. If new bonds are issued paying more, an existing bond has to become cheaper for its yield to compete. The longer the maturity, the larger that adjustment tends to be.
Is a bond fund the same as owning a bond?
No. A bond held to maturity returns its principal on a known date. A fund has no maturity date, so its unit price keeps moving with the market — the certainty of the maturity date is the thing you give up.

Conclusion

Bonds are not a better version of equities; they are a different instrument with a different job. Income that arrives on a schedule, behaviour that often diverges from stocks, and a known return of principal if you hold to maturity. Understand the type, the yield and the sensitivity to rates, and they become a tool rather than a mystery.
