FAQ

Economic calendar questions

What is an economic calendar?
An economic calendar is a schedule of the data releases and central bank announcements that move markets — inflation figures, employment reports, GDP, interest rate decisions and sentiment surveys. Each entry shows when the number is published, which currency it affects, how much impact it usually has, and the previous, forecast and actual readings so you can see whether the release beat or missed expectations.
What do the impact ratings mean?
Impact describes how much price movement a release typically causes. High-impact events such as interest rate decisions, CPI and Non-Farm Payrolls regularly move a currency by a large margin within seconds. Medium-impact events usually cause a contained move, and low-impact events are mostly of background interest. The rating reflects a typical reaction, not a guarantee — a low-impact figure can still matter if it changes the wider narrative.
What is the difference between previous, forecast and actual?
Previous is the last published reading, forecast is the consensus expectation of economists ahead of the release, and actual is the number when it lands. Markets price the forecast in advance, so the move usually comes from the gap between forecast and actual — the surprise — rather than from the absolute figure.
Are the calendar times shown in my timezone?
The calendar has its own timezone control — set it once and every release time is shown in the zone you pick. The banner clock at the top of the page stays on GMT, which is the reference most market commentary uses.
How should I trade around a high-impact release?
Carefully. Spreads widen and liquidity thins in the seconds around a major release, slippage becomes more likely, and price can spike in both directions before settling. Many traders reduce position size, widen stops or simply stand aside until the initial reaction has passed. Nothing on this page is a recommendation to trade a specific event.
Why do prices sometimes move before a release?
Markets trade expectations. Positioning shifts in the hours before a major number as traders prepare for the likely outcome, so you often see drift ahead of the release and a sharp adjustment afterwards if the data disagrees with the consensus.
Which releases matter most for the pairs I trade?
Match the release to the currency in your pair. Interest rate decisions and inflation drive the biggest moves in majors, employment data such as Non-Farm Payrolls dominates the US dollar, and commodity currencies react to Chinese data and to energy and metals inventories. Scan the currency column for the pairs you trade and note the high-impact rows first.
Is the calendar data guaranteed to be accurate?
Times, forecasts and figures are provided for general information and can be revised or rescheduled by the issuing institution at short notice.
